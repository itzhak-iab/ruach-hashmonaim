From the Editorial Desk

Editor’s Note

A new air is moving through the brigade’s battalions. The new tironim have entered the event — running under the searing sun of the Jordan Valley, training their bodies for combat — while Yonatan Battalion has emerged from Lebanon after a long and intensive period of fierce fighting against the enemy. One cycle enters and another rotates between operations. The natural movement of a brigade that renews and refreshes itself every several months.

Parshat Bamidbar sets the tribes of Israel each in their place, each beneath their banner. A whole life in the wilderness. The desert is hot and the sun beats down upon it — yet Bnei Yisrael underwent in it a long training meant to draw them out of the exile-consciousness of Egypt into a consciousness of conquering Eretz Yisrael and of upright national life of a people in its land.

Tironut, then, is a kind of Israel’s sojourn in the wilderness, while the warriors re-enact the era of Yehoshua’s wars and the conquest of the Land. For two thousand years we have studied these parshiyot under the assumption that all this belonged to the distant past — a rupture of millennia. Today we merit to stand in the very place where our fathers stood: training, fighting, and guarding the Land of our forefathers. Ashreinu sh’zachinu!

From the Commander

Commander’s Word

Capt. H
Commander of Sayeret Hashmonaim

Dear warriors, commanders and readers of the bulletin, this Shabbat we begin the reading of Chumash HaPekudim — Chumash Bamidbar — which describes the long journey of Klal Yisrael toward the Promised Land. A familiar and pressing question arises: why did the Holy One choose to give us the Torah specifically in the wilderness? Why not wait until we entered Eretz Yisrael, or alternatively, give the Torah in a more comfortable, fertile and pleasant place?

Toras HaChassidus grants us a deep perspective on this question, one that touches every one of us. The wilderness symbolizes a low and spiritually desolate place. It is an arid place that has no water — and indeed “ein mayim ela Torah” — and it is full of dangers like the “nachash, saraf v’akrav.”

The choice to grant the Torah precisely in such a place teaches us an enormous foundation: the Torah does not belong, nor does it operate, only in places of high spiritual stature. It is not limited to the walls of the beit midrash or the halls of the yeshiva. The Torah was given in the wilderness in order to prove that it penetrates and acts even in the “lowest” and most challenging places. It enables us — and demands of us — to learn it, to keep it, and to illuminate with it every place and every situation in which we find ourselves.

For us, the warriors and commanders of Sayeret Hashmonaim, this message is living and breathing within our operational reality. More than once we find ourselves in our own “wildernesses” — in penetration activities, in ambushes on darkened nights, in mud and in cold, in places light-years from the comfort of home or the holiness of the shul. Precisely there — out of the sense of mission, the brotherhood of warriors, and the grip on the eternal values of Klal Yisrael — we continue the moment of Har Sinai. We prove that the strength of our Torah and our emunah does not stop at the gate of the base, but goes with us to every point, however difficult and dark, and illuminates it. Shabbat shalom u’mevorach!

From the Tables of the Gedolei Yisrael

One’s Place, One’s Banner

HaRav HaTzaddik Rabbi Elimelech Biderman shlita

In Parshat Bamidbar the Torah sets out the order of the tribes of Israel with their banners, each tribe in its place and under its flag.

The tzaddikim explained: this parshah comes to root in a person pure and simple faith in our Father in Heaven — that all that takes place in the world is by precise hashgachah pratis. The Creator yisbarach arranges every person’s place and “banner” according to the supernal intent, and each one receives the conditions of life that fit exactly his role in the world.

I have heard many people say: “If only I had the talent of Yankel, I would learn more; if only I had money like Chatzkel, I’d support widows, orphans and the poor…” But the Holy One knows all these reckonings. He could have acted according to “your wisdom” and your counsels — yet in His great goodness He gave each one precisely what is right for his purpose and his precise place in the world.

HaRav Elimelech Biderman shlita
A Question in the Camp

A Question in the Camp

Maj. Rabbi Moshe Korkos
Brigade Rabbi · a farewell column

Q. Kvod HaRav, we understand that the Rav is parting from us. I wanted first of all to say thank you for the long period in which we merited the Rav, and for all the light the Rav cast on us over these past two years, since the founding of the brigade. As we approach this farewell — which falls precisely on the day of Chag Mattan Torah, may it come upon us for the good — we would be grateful if the Rav could give us chizuk on Torah learning, which is something I need to strengthen in. Chag HaShavuot reminds me how much my neshamah needs k’viut ittim every single day, but I need a word of chizuk on the matter. Yasher koach.

A. Shalom, tzaddik. First of all, you’ve moved me. It is a great privilege to serve in this holy brigade, and especially to see the processes from their very beginning and to see where the brigade has reached, b’ezras Hashem. This is an enormous merit. Let us not forget that first among the foundations are the dear commanders who serve in our brigade. Indeed we have merited — and of course, the heart of the matter is the warriors, for whose sake we are here.

As to your question, our Rabbis in the Gemara wrote for us: “Divrei Torah tzrichin chizuk” (Berachot 32b). And indeed, in order to succeed in Torah learning, all of us must strive for consistency and strengthening in learning — and this is not always easy.

First, sometimes we mistakenly think that Torah learning is measured by quantity — for example, how many dafim I covered or how many hours I sat. L’aniyut da’ati, the secret of k’viut ittim in learning is stability. By the nature of things, especially in the army, it’s a bit complex for learning to be stable and with concentration — particularly in our generation, when each of us receives a daily flood of information and the head isn’t always focused. How can one learn? (Shavuot is coming — a whole night straight…) What’s the tip?

Our Sages in several places recall the piercing question a person is asked in the beit din shel ma’alah: “Kava’ta ittim laTorah?” (Shabbat 31b). The Creator of the world knows the trials and complexities each of us has. He demands chiefly k’viut. Once we’ve come into the beit midrash, let us try as much as possible to learn with focus and consistency to the best of our ability — even ten minutes! The moment we define for ourselves that the first ten minutes of the learning hour are iron-clad, it will be much easier to continue. The great difficulty is usually in the first minutes of the learning hour; after we’ve already entered the learning, the continuation runs with siyata d’Shmaya.

Shlomo HaMelech says in Mishlei: “Ashrei adam shomei’a li, lishkod al daltotai yom yom…” (8:34). Torah learning is a privilege not everyone merits, and therefore we need to exploit the merit that in the brigade there is a learning hour on the schedule.

Aleh v’hatzlach. May we all merit to receive the Torah in joy and from within true unity! Shabbat shalom u’mevorach!

Faces That Tell a Story

Sgt. Eliyahu Moshe-Shlomo Tzimblist z”l

Eliyahu was born in the United States. When he was two, his family made aliyah to the city of Beit Shemesh, where he grew up and was educated.

From a young age, he stood out for an unusual talent at building things in wood — from swings and benches to cabinets and large pieces of furniture. Over the years he made and built shtenders, partitions and panels for the aron kodesh of the neighborhood shul.

His friends recall that he knew how to fix anything quickly and skillfully — always quietly and modestly, without seeking recognition or thanks. For instance, when the Rosh Yeshiva’s shtender broke, by morning it was already standing repaired, and only later did it become clear that Eliyahu was the one who had fixed it.

A line he used to say captured his way well: “Don’t wait for people to ask you for help — just help.”

Eliyahu learned at Yeshivat “Ahavat Yisrael,” and afterward at the Hesder Yeshiva “Sha’alvim.” He was a smiling and warm-hearted bachur, a true chaverman.

On 3 November 2022 he enlisted in Tzahal and served as a combat soldier in the Combat Engineering Corps, in the 601st Battalion of the “Ikvot HaBarzel” Formation (401). For him, the service was a true shlichut. He volunteered first for every mission, became deeply bonded to his comrades-in-arms, and his commanders saw in him a professional and devoted warrior.

On every leave from the army he would return to his beloved yeshiva, out of true longing for Torah learning. He dreamed that after his discharge he would return to the beit midrash, and later study building engineering to work in construction.

When the war broke out on 7 October, Eliyahu was in the middle of his training course. After he finished it, in December 2023, he entered Gaza with his battalion.

Throughout the months of fighting he was careful to reassure his family. Each time he was asked how he was, he would answer simply: “Everything is excellent, baruch Hashem.” And when his parents would say they were worried, he would add: “Don’t worry about me. Worry about the hostages, about more important things.”

On 9 Sivan 5784 (15 June 2024), during operational activity in the Tel Sultan neighborhood of Rafah, an anti-tank missile was fired at the Namer in which Eliyahu and seven other warriors were riding. All eight warriors were killed at the scene.

“We will miss you forever,” his family wrote after his fall. “We will never forget you, and we pray that the whole world will also not forget your light and your kindness.”

A Glimpse into the Parshah

A Glimpse into the Parshah

Rabbi Yehuda Pevarsky
Senior Officer for Guidance & Policy

The parshah opens with the counting of Klal Yisrael in the wilderness. On the face of it, the Torah could have simply given the total number — yet the Torah descends to particulars: each tribe, each family, each individual is counted on his own.

Rashi brings: “Out of His love for them, He counts them at every moment.” The Holy One counts Klal Yisrael out of love and importance. We can sometimes feel like “just one of all,” but the Torah teaches that every Jew is a whole world. Each one has his unique shlichut, special powers, and a role no one else can fulfill in his place.

Even in the wilderness — a place of difficulty, uncertainty and challenge — Klal Yisrael was built precisely through order, responsibility and standing together around the mission. So too with us: the combination between guarding identity and operational responsibility and doing for Klal Yisrael is a very great strength.

A soldier who goes out on a mission from a place of Torah, emunah and sense of shlichut brings with him a special power that is measured not only in physical strength, but also in spiritual strength.

Chazal say: “Ish al diglo b’otot” — each tribe had its banner, its color and its uniqueness, but all together were part of one camp of Klal Yisrael. So too in the Hashmonaim Brigade: each one comes from a different background, a different style and a different community, but all of us act together for one goal — to guard Klal Yisrael out of kiddush Shem Shamayim.

In conclusion, we wish to bless and offer great thanks to the outgoing Brigade Rabbi, HaRav Moshe Korkos, for a tenure of doing, devotion and accompaniment of soldiers and commanders with a shining face, responsibility and faith. We wish him very great success in his next role — may he continue to spread Torah, kedushah and good influence in every place he goes.

And we also bless the incoming Brigade Rabbi, HaRav Betzalel Givoni, with blessing for success and abundant siyata d’Shmaya. May it be His will that he merit to lead the brigade, to strengthen the world of Torah among the soldiers, and to see great blessing in all the work of his hands. Shabbat shalom u’mevorach.

Shteiging in the Brigade

Shteiging in the Brigade

Rabbi Pinchas Yosef Akrav
Camp / Brigade Beit Midrash

When Gdud Yonatan was formed this year, it was stationed at Camp Keren in the Golan Heights. From the outset, three weeks before the battalion’s entry, a “vanguard force” from the battalion arrived to prepare the base as the home of the battalion’s warriors.

As is well known, wherever the Hashmonaim soldiers set foot, the first thing they build is a firm infrastructure of Torah learning. The battalion Rabbi, HaRav Aryeh Rubinstein, sought to strengthen the Torah-learning sedarim of the vanguard force, and arranged three tactical-equipment vouchers, 500 NIS each, for a raffle among those who came to learn. Every hour of learning earned the learner one raffle ticket.

In the end the raffle was held — and the one who won it was an Ag”m officer.

Anyone who understands a little about the complexity of staff work knows that the Ag”m officer is the busiest man on staff — on whose shoulders rests the full responsibility for coordinating and planning all the unit’s trainings and activities. This very Ag”m officer (B.K.) would rise every morning at five in order to engage in Torah, learning Gemara as part of Daf HaYomi. Like the early tzaddikim, he learns every morning for one hour before tefillah — iron! He doesn’t budge from his commitment to k’viut ittim; he knows that from the moment after Shacharit he will be sunk in the work of the battalion, so he dedicated the morning to the “business” of Torah in quality — this aside from closing the day in the night hours with additional learning b’chavruta.

By the way — he refused to accept the prize he had won, saying he prefers to be a servant who attends his master not in order to receive a reward.

These are the officers of Hashmonaim — true bnei Torah!

From a Warrior’s Diary

From a Warrior’s Diary

A Recruit at the Bahad

This is the second week. If in the first week we were still in a kind of shock — from the uniforms, from the distance, from the thought that “here, it’s really happening” — the second week is the week in which the dust begins to settle, and reality begins to be built.

I look around me in the hallway of the barracks and see the faces. These aren’t just the faces of friends from the study-hall bench or from the neighborhood; these are the faces of the people in whose hands, in a few months, I will place my life — and they will place theirs in mine. There is something in this intensity, in this connection that is created without words, that simply cannot be explained to someone who stayed outside.

This week was not easy. The discipline, the precision in times, the standing on your feet when all you want is to sit a moment with a sefer or a cup of coffee in peace. But within that running, within that “Ken HaMefaked,” something amazing happens: we learn what it means to be part of something greater than ourselves.

Sometimes, when I stand in chatzi goren and look up at the skies of the Bahad, I think about the name of our brigade. Hashmonaim. This is not just a brand. This is an inheritance. We are not here to become something else — we are here to be the fighting version of who we are. We bring with us tefillah, the spirit, the values from home, and pour them into the iron of the weapon and the dust of the field.

At nights, when finally the lights go out, there is a moment of quiet. That is the moment when the longing for home floats up a little, the thought of the coming Shabbat or of the family. But then I remember why I came here. I came to prove that one can — and must — be both: both Chareidi who keeps the light as the heavy, and a professional warrior who doesn’t give himself a millimeter.

The second week in tironut is the week when we stop being “new inductees” and start being “soldiers.” We’re not there yet — the road is still long — but the first steps are already plowed in the earth of the Bahad.

Chevreh, lift your heads. We are writing history here, even if right now it looks like just another day of standing in the schedule. We are Hashmonaim soldiers — that is the greatest pride of our generation.

A Story for Shabbat

A Story for Shabbat

Capt. Rabbi Aryeh Rubinstein
Rabbi of Gdud Yonatan

The first Rashi in our parshah speaks of how, out of the Holy One’s great love for us, He “counts us every hour.” How much we must strengthen in ahavat Yisrael.

The Baron Rothschild held a Kiddusha Rabbah for all the inhabitants of the city, on the occasion of a special simchah in his home. When everyone arrived at the magnificent hall, they were astonished to see that there was no food — only goblets and bottles of wine. While everyone was trying to understand what was going on, the Baron entered, took the goblet in his hand, made Kiddush over the wine, and immediately afterward made “Borei minei mezonot” and ate the goblet. It turned out that special cakes had been prepared in the shape of a goblet — a flourish of the Baron in honor of the occasion.

At the place of honor sat the Gaon Rabbi Chaim Ozer Grodzinski zt”l, and the Baron honored him to make Kiddush again to exempt all the assembled. The Grach”O delayed several minutes, and only afterward did he too make Kiddush over the wine and eat the goblet.

Afterward several Jews approached the Grach”O and asked the Rav why he had not made Kiddush immediately. The tzaddik answered them: From the strict letter of the law it was a problem to make Kiddush before covering the mezonot — presumably a challenging matter in this situation. On the other hand, if I did not make Kiddush, the Baron would surely be embarrassed by this. Then I thought to myself that the source of this halacha stems from “so that the bread will not see its shame.” If so, the matter is simple and clear that it is certainly preferable to embarrass the bread than to embarrass a Jew, chas v’shalom!

Life in the Brigade

Life in the Brigade

Yochanan Chayot (Chayet) · Incoming Company R”M

Tell us about yourself.

My name is Yochanan Chayet (Chayot), 36, married plus four children, b’li ayin hara. My father mori is HaRav Baruch Chayet shlita, Rosh Yeshivat Maarava, and a well-known figure in the world of education. He is also a composer and creator, and many of his songs are nichsei tzon barzel of the Jewish people (such as “Kol Ha’Olam Kulo,” “Mi Ha’Ish,” “Lo Amut Ki Echyeh,” and more). I learned at Yeshiva Gedolah Mir, and got married. I became an R”M at Yeshivat Maarava and a “Sho’el U’Meshiv” at another yeshiva in Moshav Matityahu. I taught Torah all day.

How did you come to the brigade?

A combination of circumstances led me to it. The Seventh of October gave me a great awakening to understand that we cannot live in a bubble and cannot be disconnected from what is happening here in the Land to our people. I saw firsthand my brother who is in miluim in the Alexandroni Brigade — hundreds of days far from family and home — and I saw how his wife and children deal with it. The cry of the verse “Shall your brothers go out to war and you sit here?” grated on me very deeply. How could I sit at home while my brother gives himself for the people? I was certainly doing shlichut and teaching, but it felt to me that something was lacking in me, at least personally. Then I learned of the Hashmonaim Brigade, through my dear brother-in-law HaRav Aryeh Rubinstein hy”v, who began as a soldier in the miluim track of the first cycle, and today serves as Rabbi of Yonatan Battalion in the brigade. I heard from him and from a few others about the possibility of enlisting as a soldier, and decided I was joining as a soldier. Baruch Hashem, I merited to finish combat training, was a squad leader there, and I don’t regret it for a moment. They wanted me to continue to the Machimim course, but it was hard to continue right after training to Machim — right now I need a bit of a respite. Perhaps later I’ll come back as a Mem-Kaf. In any case, I’ll soon return to the brigade as a company R”M in the battalion, and we’ll see what comes next.

Tell us about the transition from being an R”M in yeshiva to being a recruit in combat training.

Oof… first of all, it’s a great lesson in humility and lowness of spirit. I’m 36, and I receive orders from someone 20 years old who puts me at “matzav shtayim” and runs me back and forth because some small thing wasn’t exactly up to standard… You have to lower the head and understand that there’s a bigger event going on here, and keep in mind the great vision of “why I’m here” — and not get stuck in those small moments that can humiliate and feel unpleasant. When you understand the reason and the context, it gives strength. But it was certainly a powerful experience. I felt I received an enormous amount from it personally, and I felt shlichut at every moment. It was clear to me what we are doing here — both at the general level, to bear the yoke with one’s fellow with Klal Yisrael, and specifically the shlichut we are doing in the brigade. I knew and felt how much we are doing something historic for Klal Yisrael. I very much believe we can make a deep change — that our public too, for whom this is relevant of course, will take responsibility for Klal Yisrael, and that we will be partners in this immense mitzvah of “nosei b’ol im chavero” and “lo ta’amod al dam re’echa.” May we merit to do this in kedushah and in taharah.

You speak about bearing the yoke with one’s fellow — just this week you released a new song that speaks about this very theme.

Indeed, this week I merited to release another single. I’ve been engaged in music for several years, but this week I released a song I had the privilege of writing during training. It is essentially a song that expresses what we are doing here in the brigade — that partnership, the sense of putting down a little of the desire always to be right and to win, to listen a little and understand the other side, and to try to connect with one another.

I have a friend who is a company commander in Yahalom, and he invited me to speak before his company when I was in the middle of training. These are the salt of the earth — an elite combat unit, who pass screening after screening to get there. Truly good and sweet guys. I told them a bit of my story and my background, and spoke about the importance of unity. One of them tells me that he had been in a pre-army mechina, and they brought in avreichim to speak with them, so they would get to know the Chareidi world. And the avreich tells him: “If we go to the army, it’s going to Gehinnom; it’s impossible for us.” The bachur asks me — how am I supposed to have a dialogue with a person like that? Someone who tells me the army is Gehinnom for him, and even worse? What do we have in common? I thought a little and asked him: when you come to speak with this person, what is your goal — what do you want to get out of the conversation? He answered me: I want them to share in the burden that all the citizens here share in. I told him: l’aniyut da’ati I think this is where the problem lies. Because you come with an agenda, to try to convince him to enlist. You need to come with a clean heart for one thing: to connect with the person. To try to understand his pain, his perspective. The starting point is that we are one people, and we want first to understand what hurts the other — without any desire to change. Just to understand, to listen, to accept and to connect. After you do that, only then is there a chance for change and dialogue.

You wrote in the song “B’or Panecha lo neida od milchamah.” Did you write that after your fighting in Gaza?

Truthfully, in that line I was referring to the inner wars between us. Throughout our history, sadly, Klal Yisrael has continually fallen on this. If we merit to true peace from within, we won’t know any more war from one to another — and then truly we won’t have wars from outside either. As the Gemara says: when David went out to war, because there was strife and lashon hara among Israel, they fell in battle; whereas in the time of Achav, despite there being idol worshippers, since there was unity among them, they won in battle.

How would you summarize the experience in the brigade?

We started only a year and a half ago, and it’s still a kind of pilot. But I see that we’re on a positive trend and that this is really going in the right direction. There is what to improve and fix — nothing is perfect from the first moment. But it is agreed upon among all of us that there is an inner spirit of kedushah pulsing inside the Bahad, something beyond merely technical. We feel here a deeper shlichut in what we are doing. There is a sense that something very big is happening here in the right direction. It is a great privilege to be a partner in this doing. The meticulousness on tefillot, on the learning hour, the very concept of company R”Mim — this is truly a yeshiva inside Tzahal.

A yeshiva inside Tzahal? Not an overstated phrase?

I’m full of hope that this won’t be exaggerated at all. A young bachur who comes into the Bahad is — one way or another — being educated to something. If we don’t take initiative and bequeath the pure hashkafah, they will be built in another way. From my perspective, unequivocally I see this as a yeshiva that is entirely avodat Hashem.

As a company R”M, what message would you want to convey to the soldier?

I’m specifically going to be in the battalion and not in the Bahad. From the experience I managed to taste a little on the Lebanon border for a few weeks, going in and out for activities, I understood that life in the battalion is much more of a mess. The schedule is much less ordered, the learning hour is not always orderly, and naturally a soldier with disorganized times somewhat loses himself. I think one of the things that’s very important to bequeath is that the soldier should know that even if the schedule isn’t very orderly, and he doesn’t pray l’chatchilah at the right time, and he doesn’t have an orderly learning hour — he should know that he is in the framework of avodat Hashem. That everything he is doing is kodesh. Don’t let him feel about himself, “vai, I’ve fallen, I’m not in the schedule, I’m no longer who I once was…” No. This is your shlichut now — these are the circumstances you’re in, and you need to take the light you have within this place and rejoice in it, grow in it. Now this is your avodat Hashem.

A special moment you remember from training?

Just last week, at the ceremony marking the end of training. Since the war with Iran prevented the original beret march at the Kotel, they did a make-up march for us — with the families — and we arrived at the Kotel HaMa’aravi at the end. That moment took me back to our first week of training, when they brought us to watch the closing ceremony of the cycle before us; and I’ll tell you the truth, I had my doubts I would make it. Standing opposite the Kotel last week, remembering those thoughts — we did it. It was surreal. It gave me belief that with a goal, a vision and the will, one can succeed — even at this age.

Maj. Yitzchak Barkai

Sha’ar HaGai — The Passage to the Mountain

The Memory within the Joy

This week we mark the day on which we merited to return to the Kotel HaMa’aravi and to the Old City of Jerusalem. It is only fitting toward our chain of generations to pause and, in the moment of joy, recall those who sowed in tears. “Ha’zor’im b’dim’ah, b’rinah yiktzoru.” The reapers of 5727 reaped seeds that had been sown over two thousand years of exile. Everyone who said “L’shanah Ha’ba’ah b’Yerushalayim” and did not merit. Everyone who gave his soul on the way to Jerusalem throughout the generations. All the dreamers who died in foreign lands with their faces eastward — they are all the sowers. The paratroopers at the Kotel were the final reapers. And as is known, “a mitzvah is named only after the one who completes it.”

And not by chance, the 28th of Iyar is also the memorial day for the Jews of Ethiopia who perished on their way to the Land. Perhaps to teach us that there cannot be the “rinah of the reapers” without the “tear of the sowers.” The Jews of Ethiopia crossed the deserts of Sudan on their way to Jerusalem out of an eternal yearning, two thousand years old, that pushed and continues to push all of us toward that same Zion-point in Jerusalem. Many of them remained on the way. And when we, their continuers, walk today in Jerusalem, we are continuing the lives of seeds sown in tears in the Sudanese desert.

The Neck Between the Lowlands and the Mountain

During the drive up to Jerusalem, in one moment the landscape changes. The level lowlands remain behind, and the mountains begin to close in from every side. We know this passage by the seemingly mundane name “Mechalef Sha’ar HaGai.” But to anyone who listens to the echoes rising up from between the rocks, it is clear that this is not just another stretch of road.

The historical and spiritual struggle over the way to Jerusalem is woven also into the geographical structure of the Land. The topography changes markedly at the meeting between the lowland region and the central mountain ridge. The free spaces of movement on the plain are channeled into narrow valleys. The most prominent of them is Sha’ar HaGai, which functions both geographically and conceptually similar to the human neck. Just as the heart pumps the blood and pushes it upward to the head through the narrow passage of the neck, so the movement is channeled from the broad lowlands into the valley, in order to ascend to the “head” — Jerusalem. The ascent to the mountain requires a physical passage through a narrow place.

Because of its being “a necessary passage,” the space of connection between the lowlands and the mountain has from time immemorial been a strategic focus. In this space, Yehoshua bin Nun commanded “Shemesh b’Givon dom, v’yarei’ach b’Emek Ayalon,” and at Sha’ar HaGai he pursued the five kings as they fled to the cave at Makedah, near Beit Shemesh. Later, in the battle of Emmaus, Yehuda the Maccabee shrewdly exploited the terrain to strike the enemy camp — a victory that was a critical milestone in clearing the way for the purification of the Mikdash. Generations later, in the War of Independence (5708), Bab el-Wad became a symbol of the struggle for Klal Yisrael’s connection to its capital, Jerusalem — here the supply convoys entered the pass again and again under fire to break through the blockades and stream air into the cut-off city. The rusting armored vehicles standing to this day at the roadside are not a monument — they are the seeds from which Jerusalem as we know it today grew.

The Sowers and the Reapers

Jerusalem of 5727 grew from two thousand years of tears. The paratroopers at the Kotel drew their strength from the fighters of 5708 who struggled for Klal Yisrael’s connection to Jerusalem, from the prayers of the Jews of Sefarad, from the longings of the Chassidim of Poland, and from the singing of the Mekubalim in Tzfat. All of them sowed. They did not know that they would not reap. What we see today in the news is always either the fruit of a distant sowing, or a seed that will be reaped in generations to come. And this is the secret of Israeli history: our responsibility is only for the climbing itself, not for the question of whether we will see the summit.

To ascend in the valley. To cross the narrow place. To sow something — even in tears, even without knowing who will reap. Shabbat shalom!

A Burning Chassidic Soldier

A Burning Chassidic Soldier

Cpl. Mendel Rota
Gdud Yonatan

That scene always amuses me. New tironim in their first weeks. I see them running, climbing up onto the rope for the first time, doing a few push-ups, and shouting “Misparei barzel l’hitpaked.” It takes me back. You can think how far back — only a year ago I finished training. But it feels like ten years back.

How I ran after some young commander who ran me back and forth to touch some wall. How I shouted all kinds of strange phrases while doing twenty push-ups: “I… am happy… very… to serve… the Jewish people… and I promise… not to be lazy… but…” Ana arif what they got us muttering down there. Avadim hayinu. What a period, achi.

But now, in the last two months, my whole company has been in Lebanon. They’ve done crazy work. Purged whole areas of the enemy, cleaned out weapons stockpiles, captured terrorists, real work. Warriors at the top of their game. And I think of all these new tironim: in another six months or so they’ll be there. Becoming real warriors. Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, Judea and Samaria. Taking responsibility for our state.

That gap between the bitul ha’yesh you go through in tironut and the great power you receive from the moment you become warriors. And what bitul ha’yesh! Tironut is another planet. From the moment you step off the shuttle on Sunday, you simply become a complete robot. Up until the moment you go out at the end of a week or two. A crazy experience.

And how much every little thing in tironut teaches you for life. I’m reminded of my “period of forgetfulness” at the start of tironut. I’d forget the weapon in the room. They shouted, they punished, but it took me time to feel the weapon as if it was part of my hands. Once I even forgot the weapon in the room while in the middle of a reprimand from my commander on this very matter. I told him I was running to take a shower — and forgot the weapon like that in the room. All my friends in the room burst out laughing. In the end, when they saw that punishments weren’t helping, I got a final warning from the platoon commander that I needed to come up with a creative idea/punishment that I knew would scare me. Otherwise they couldn’t entrust me with a weapon. I had already planned a few especially creative things. One of them was that every time I forgot the weapon, I’d post to all my followers that I forgot the weapon. I was even afraid to propose the idea to the commanders. I kept the idea inside myself until the next talk with the platoon commander. Somehow, from then on, I no longer forgot the weapon.

I remember another story, at the firing range. How important it is there that the weapon not be pointed, chas v’shalom, to the sides. But by my nature I drift a little. Once, my weapon was a little tilted to the side. I suddenly caught an enormous shout from one of the commanders: “Mendel!! Get out!!!” Boom. I went red and white. Embarrassments! I was the oldest of the bunch, and I walked out of the range like a humiliated child. But from that day on, I no longer accidentally moved the weapon to the side.

Today I walk with the weapon in the street, in full confidence. The hand on the stock — this is the weapon that protects me. I don’t forget it; no mistakes with such a tool. This is my baby. With this tool I defend my people wherever I go. With this tool I know how to neutralize a terrorist wherever I get to. With this tool I make kiddush Hashem, when people see a Chareidi soldier carrying his weapon with Jewish pride. All those shouts — they straightened out all the crookedness of my heart. “Lo nivre’u re’amim ela l’fashet akmimiyut sheba’lev,” says the Gemara in Berachot.

I’m telling this for the new tironim, who have suddenly landed inside the event. We, the first cycle, had no concept of what tironut was. As if I’d been tossed into some movie I had no idea what was happening in — while someone above me kept shouting at me what role I was supposed to play. That was the event.

But it won’t be long, and you’ll suddenly grasp what has grown within you during this short period. It looks like a lot of time but it really isn’t. Think about what you accomplished in the seven months before the army. You moved a little to the right and a little to the left, that’s all. You read maybe two books, met three new people, killed time. Now your time is exploited to the level of seconds. This is a period that will accompany you all your life.

Kitzer — what I came to say. It came out as a riff of a corporal getting carried away with himself, never mind — you are awfully sweet, and it’s moving to see you. You have the responsibility to shape the next Hashmonaim generation. Every cycle influences the cycle after it, just as you receive a lot from the tradition set here by the previous cycles. But we are still in the shaping stage. Take responsibility — shape the figure of the Hashmonaim warrior. We want to win this national mission we’ve taken upon ourselves. It’s we who will determine whether Hashmonaim succeeds or not. Go for it with all your strength. Gut Shabbes.

A Night Awake: The Implication for Birchot HaTorah

Q. On the night of Chag HaShavuot it is customary to remain awake all night in Torah learning. May one recite Birchot HaTorah the next morning as usual?

A.

The Gemara in Berachot (11b) states: “Rav Yehuda said in the name of Shmuel: One who rose early to learn — before he recited Shema, he needs to bless; after he recited Shema, he doesn’t need to bless, because he was already exempted with Ahavah Rabbah.” What does one bless? Rav Yehuda in the name of Shmuel: “Asher kideshanu b’mitzvotav v’tzivanu la’asok b’divrei Torah”; Rabbi Yochanan adds: “Ha’arev na…”; Rav Hamnuna adds: “Asher bachar banu mi’kol ha’amim v’natan lanu et torato. Baruch atah Hashem, notein ha’Torah.” Rav Hamnuna said: This is the choicest of the blessings, therefore let us say them all.

The Rosh (Berachot 1:11) discusses: Until when does the blessing one made apply? He concludes that for people who are constantly engaged in Torah, and even when they go out to their affairs they hurry to attend to their needs in order to return to learn, and their mind is always on their learning — this is not considered an interruption. He adds that therefore one who learns at night doesn’t need to bless; conversely one who slept by day — this is an interruption and he needs to bless again. He also brings the opinion of Rabbeinu Tam that a daytime nap is not an interruption.

The Shulchan Aruch (47:11) rules: “A fixed nap during the day on his bed is an interruption; and some say it is not an interruption, and so is the custom.” The Mishnah Berurah there brings many acharonim who rule one should bless, and concludes that there is what to rely on for both customs.

The Magen Avraham explains: the Tur and the Rosh hold that everything depends on sleep; Rabbeinu Tam holds that the morning blessings were instituted because the Sages fixed this blessing for each day.

HaGaon Rav Asher Weiss sharpens that according to the Tur and the Rosh, Birchot HaTorah are of the type of Birchot HaMitzvot on the mitzvah of Talmud Torah, therefore dependent on “interruption.” By contrast, the view of Rabbeinu Tam is that these are blessings of praise, like Birchot HaShachar. The Gr”a (Rav Akiva Eiger) innovates that in the case where one slept by day and remained awake the whole night, all agree he is obligated in the blessing in the morning — and so ruled the Mishnah Berurah (s.k. 28).

Summary

“One who is awake all night, according to the custom of many of the Ashkenazim, should not recite Birchot HaTorah in the morning (and only if possible, one should fulfill them by hearing the blessings from someone else, or have intent to be exempted by the blessing of Ahavat Olam [Ahavah Rabbah]), unless he had a fixed nap during the day (before that night or after it). The custom of the Sephardim is to recite the blessings, while the custom of the Yemenites and some of the Ashkenazim is to try to fulfill the blessing by hearing it from another; and when this is not possible, they bless themselves.” (Torat HaMachaneh, ch. 4:4)
